## Why spectroscopy matters

Without spectroscopy, astronomy would be limited largely to images of brightness and position. Spectroscopy turns light into **measurable information** about distant objects.

---

## Types of spectra (Kirchhoff’s laws)

| Type | Description | Origin |
|:-----|:------------|:-------|
| **Continuous** | Smooth range of colors without gaps | Hot, dense objects (e.g. a star’s deep layers) |
| **Absorption** | Dark lines on a continuous background | Light passing through cooler, thinner gas |
| **Emission** | Bright lines at specific wavelengths on a dark background | Hot, low-density gas (e.g. some nebulae) |

## Spectral lines and elements

Each chemical element absorbs and emits light at characteristic wavelengths — like **fingerprints**. By studying those lines, astronomers can identify hydrogen, helium, iron, and more, even across vast distances.

---

## Temperature and spectroscopy

The shape of a spectrum and the strength of its lines depend strongly on temperature:

- **Hotter stars** emit more light at shorter (bluer) wavelengths  
- **Cooler stars** emit more at longer (redder) wavelengths  

This ties directly to **star classification** and placing stars on the **H–R diagram**.

---

## The Doppler effect

Motion shifts spectral lines:

- **Blueshift** — object moving toward us → lines shift to shorter wavelengths  
- **Redshift** — object moving away → lines shift to longer wavelengths  

{: .important }
This effect is essential for detecting many exoplanets and for measuring the expansion of the universe.

---

## Why spectroscopy is essential

Spectroscopy helps determine:

1. Chemical composition  
2. Temperature  
3. Radial velocity (motion along the line of sight)  
4. Clues to evolutionary stage  

**Light is not only brightness — it is information.** Spectroscopy is how we read it.
