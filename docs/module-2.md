## Why do we classify stars?

When we look at the night sky, stars may seem similar. In reality, stars differ in:

- Temperature  
- Brightness  
- Size  
- Age  

Classification helps astronomers organize stars and understand how they live and evolve — similar to how biologists classify living things.

---

## What is star classification?

Star classification groups stars by physical properties, mainly:

- Surface temperature  
- Luminosity (true brightness)  
- Color  

These properties are related, not independent.

---

## Temperature and color of stars

A star’s color tells us about its surface temperature.

- **Blue stars** → very hot  
- **White stars** → hot  
- **Yellow stars** → medium temperature  
- **Red stars** → cooler  

Astronomers often use **color indices** because color is practical to measure from observations.

![Star Classification by Color](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/image/2506/25BrightestStars_Jittasaiyapan_960.jpg)

---

## Luminosity vs brightness

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Brightness** | How bright a star appears from Earth |
| **Luminosity** | How much energy a star actually produces |

Two stars can look equally bright in the sky but have very different luminosities. The HR Diagram uses **luminosity** (or absolute magnitude), not only apparent brightness.

---

## What is the Hertzsprung–Russell (HR) Diagram?

The HR Diagram relates:

- **Horizontal axis:** temperature or color  
- **Vertical axis:** luminosity or absolute magnitude  

It is one of the most important tools in astronomy.

![HR DIAGRAM](https://chandra.harvard.edu/edu/formal/stellar_ev/story/hr.jpg)

---

## Why the HR Diagram is powerful

When astronomers plotted many stars, they found:

- Stars did not fill the diagram at random  
- Clear **patterns and regions** appeared  

Those patterns help us infer:

- What type of star we are looking at  
- How it generates energy  
- Which stage of life it is in  

---

## Main regions of the HR Diagram

### 1. Main sequence

- Most stars fall here (including the Sun)  
- Stars fuse hydrogen into helium  
- Higher mass → hotter and brighter; lower mass → cooler and dimmer  

### 2. Giants and supergiants

- Very luminous; often cooler than the hottest main-sequence stars  
- Large in size  
- Later stages of stellar life after significant hydrogen use  

### 3. White dwarfs

- Hot but faint  
- Very small  
- Dense leftover cores that cool over time  

---

## Why stars form these regions

A star’s place on the diagram depends on mass, composition, and evolutionary stage. Physics limits where stars can sit stably — that is why we see **bands**, not random scatter.

---

## Stellar evolution and motion on the diagram

Stars **move** on the HR Diagram over time. For example, a low-mass star may:

1. Spend a long time on the main sequence  
2. Expand toward the giant region  
3. End as a white dwarf  

The diagram is a map of stellar life cycles, not a static catalog.

---

## How astronomers use the HR Diagram

- Classify unknown stars  
- Estimate ages (especially in clusters)  
- Study stellar populations  
- Connect observation to theory
